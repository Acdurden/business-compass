Every acquisition

Read these records carefully. The loan is not the purchase price: buyers also put in at least 10% cash and sellers often carry a note, so prices run above the loan. Only SBA-financed deals appear; cash and private-equity deals do not. The lender chooses the "Change of Ownership" label, so partner buyouts coded differently are missing and counts run low. The borrower name is usually the buyer's company, often formed for the purchase. Where one buyer took two loans on the same day, they are counted as one acquisition.
How to use it with a client. "Since 2017, … businesses like yours were bought with SBA loans. The typical loan was …, and these are the banks that made them." It shows the owner that a financed buyer exists for a firm their size, and which lenders a buyer would call.

Small deals, by industry

Businesses sold on BizBuySell, Q3 2021 to Q2 2026. Earnings here are seller's discretionary earnings, which include the owner's pay, so these multiples run lower than EBITDA multiples.

What these figures are. They are ranges published by M&A advisors and surveys of dealmakers. None is a record of closed sales, and each source draws its size groups in different places. FE International and Palmer are sell-side firms publishing guidance for agency owners. The 15%, 20% and 25% margins are the three levels FE International names: below 15% reads as margin risk to a buyer, 20% is the level buyers look for, and above 25% shows operating leverage.
How to use it with a client. "At your margin, the multiple buyers pay starts stepping up at about … of revenue and again at about …. Growing past those points raises the price twice: more earnings, and a higher multiple on all of them."

What it would cost a buyer to hire someone to do the owner's job. Advisors use this to adjust the owner's own pay when working out EBITDA. Source: Bureau of Labor Statistics wage survey, May 2025.

Market pay

Adjustment to EBITDA

If the owner is paid less than the market rate, a buyer who hires a replacement pays more, so the business earns less than it appears. The reverse applies if the owner is overpaid.

…

Limits. These are wages only. Benefits and payroll taxes, which an employer pays on top, are not included. BLS does not publish figures where the sample is too small or pay is above its reporting cap, so some ranges stop short. Pay by industry is national only.

What gets built

  1. A quarterly download job. Runs automatically from your GitHub account a month after each quarter ends, when the SBA posts new records. It downloads the SBA loan files, keeps only acquisitions in our industries, and loads them into the app. Once a year it does the same for the BLS wage survey. Prime rate is checked weekly.
  2. Three new lists in the database: acquisitions, market pay, and rates. Each row keeps the date it was downloaded, so the history is never overwritten.
  3. This Market data page for advisors and admins.
  4. Two cards on each client's advisor page: "Comparable acquisitions" (same industry, same state, last three years) and "Owner pay check" (the owner's stated pay against market pay for the role and place).

Nothing here changes a score, a valuation or anything a client sees.

Worth adding later

  1. Census industry benchmarks: revenue and payroll per employee by industry and state, to check whether a client's figures look normal for its size. Needs a free Census key.
  2. Public-company acquisitions from SEC filings: real prices when a listed company buys an agency. Rare and slow to collect.
  3. The published multiples already designed on the Insights preview.

Decisions for you

  1. Which industries to track. The preview has eight groups; agencies are the default.
  2. Whether borrower names show to all advisors. They are public records, but they are named businesses.
  3. Whether a client-page card should ever be shown to a client, or stay advisor-only.
